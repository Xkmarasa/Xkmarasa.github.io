Studies · Holy Shroud

The Holy Shroud

A linen cloth that shows, front and back, the image of a man who suffered the torments of a crucifixion. It is the most studied archaeological object in the world and it still raises questions.

  1. Home
  2. /The Holy Shroud

What it is

The Holy Shroud (also called the Sindone, from the Greek sindon, linen cloth) is a linen cloth about four and a half metres long and just over a metre wide. On it, very faintly, one can see the front and back image of an adult man with crossed hands, laid head to head in the middle of the cloth.

Tradition identifies it with the burial cloth in which Joseph of Arimathea wrapped the body of Jesus, as the Gospels tell. The Catholic Church makes no pronouncement on its authenticity, which it leaves to science, but venerates it as an icon of the Passion: Saint John Paul II called it a “mirror of the Gospel”.

What the image shows

The figure bears marks consistent with the accounts of the Passion: scourge wounds all over the body, head injuries compatible with a crown of thorns, wounds in the wrists and feet, and a wound in the side from which blood and a clearer fluid flow.

The image is superficial: it affects only the outermost fibres of the linen, and no pigment explains it. The bloodstains, by contrast, do soak into the fabric and, according to several studies, were laid down before the image.

A history documented since the 14th century

The first reliable record places the cloth in Lirey, in the French region of Champagne, in the mid-14th century, in the hands of the knight Geoffroi de Charny. In 1453 it passed to the House of Savoy, which kept it in Chambéry.

In 1532 a fire in the Chambéry chapel badly damaged it: the heat melted part of the silver reliquary and left the burns and patches still visible today, sewn on by the Poor Clare nuns in 1534. In 1578 the Shroud reached Turin, where it remains. In 1983 the last King of Italy, Umberto II, bequeathed it to the Pope.

For the centuries before Lirey there are several hypotheses linking it to images venerated in the East, such as the Mandylion of Edessa; these are open lines of research and historians do not agree on them.

The 1898 photographic negative

In 1898 the lawyer and photographer Secondo Pia took the first photograph of the Holy Shroud. When he developed the plate he found that the negative showed a positive image, far sharper and with unexpected depth: the image on the cloth behaves like a negative.

That discovery opened the modern scientific study of the Shroud, which today brings together historians, forensic doctors, physicists, chemists and textile specialists.

Scientific research

In 1978 a multidisciplinary American team (STURP) examined the cloth for five days. It concluded that the image is not a painting and that the stains are blood, without explaining how the image was formed.

In 1988 three laboratories (Oxford, Zurich and Arizona) radiocarbon-dated a sample from one corner and obtained dates between 1260 and 1390. The result is still debated: it is questioned whether the sample was representative of the whole cloth, since it came from a handled and possibly repaired area. Later research using other methods has proposed different dates, and there is no consensus today.

Since the 2002 restoration the Holy Shroud has been kept lying flat in a case filled with inert gas, in a chapel of Turin Cathedral. It is shown to the public only on extraordinary occasions.

Key facts

Where it is
Cathedral of Saint John the Baptist, Turin (Italy)
Size
About 4.4 × 1.1 metres
Fabric
Linen in herringbone weave (3:1 twill)
First reliable record
Lirey (France), around 1355
In Turin since
1578
Owner
The Holy See, since 1983
